Choosing a web development company in Pakistan is less about finding the most impressive portfolio and more about finding someone who will build what you need, in your name, and still answer your messages six months after launch. Many of the problems that surface after launch are not about design. They are about a domain registered in the developer's name, a site nobody updates, or a project that drifted because nothing was written down.

So the checks that matter most are practical ones: look at live sites rather than screenshots, make sure you will own the domain, hosting, code and accounts, tie payments to milestones you can see, and agree in writing what happens after launch. This guide turns those checks into a list you can work through with any developer, freelancer or agency.

## Start with your own written brief

Before you contact anyone, write one page that answers:

- What the site is for (enquiries, bookings, sales, information) and how you will know it is working.
- Who it is for, and what they need to find or do.
- The pages and features you think you need, and which are essential for launch.
- What content you already have (text, photos, product lists) and what someone else must produce.
- Accounts you already own: domain, hosting, email, Google Business Profile, social pages.
- Your budget range and any fixed deadline.

Send the same brief to everyone. Their replies become comparable, and the questions they ask back tell you a lot. A good developer will push back on unclear parts. A weak one will send a price within the hour without asking anything. ## Freelancer vs agency

Both can be the right choice. The question is what your project needs.

| | Freelancer | Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | Small, well-defined sites; a fixed task on an existing site | Projects that need design, development, content and marketing together |
| Strengths | Direct contact with the person doing the work; often more flexible | Several skills under one roof; cover when someone is away |
| Risks | One person is a single point of failure: illness, a full-time job offer, or simply moving on | You may deal with a salesperson, not the people building your site |
| Ask | "What happens to my project if you are unavailable for two weeks?" | "Who exactly will work on my project, and can I talk to them?" |

Whichever you choose, the ownership, contract and support checks below apply equally.

## Check the portfolio properly

A portfolio of screenshots proves very little. Ask for links to live sites, then spend ten minutes on each.

1. **Open it on your phone, on mobile data.** Google now [indexes the mobile version of sites](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/10/mobile-first-is-here), and that is how most of your visitors will see yours too. Check that text is readable, buttons are easy to tap and nothing spills off the screen.
2. **Run it through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/).** Google's tool shows lab results and, for sites with enough visitors, [field data from real Chrome users](https://developers.google.com/speed/docs/insights/v5/about). Google's ["good" thresholds](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) are a Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less, Interaction to Next Paint of 200 milliseconds or less, and Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1 or less. One slow site isn't damning, since the client may have added heavy content later. A portfolio full of them is a pattern.
3. **Test the forms and buttons.** Send a test enquiry. Tap the WhatsApp button. Add something to the basket on a store. A broken contact route can lose you enquiries without anyone noticing.
4. **Look for variety.** If every site looks the same, you are probably buying a template with your logo on it. That can be fine, but you should know.
5. **Ask what they actually did.** "Design and build", "build only from someone else's design" and "maintenance" are very different claims.
6. **Speak to one past client.** Ask whether the project ran to time, whether the price changed, and whether the developer still responds.

## Make sure you own the domain, hosting, code and accounts

This is the most important section in this guide. If a relationship ends badly, ownership decides whether you walk away with your website or start again.

**Domain.** The domain should be registered with you, or your business, as the registrant. For .com and other generic domains, [ICANN Lookup](https://lookup.icann.org/) shows registration data and the registrar, though some contact details may be redacted. ICANN's rules say the [registered name holder is the only party that can approve a transfer](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/name-holder-faqs-2017-10-10-en), which is exactly why the name on the record matters. For .pk domains, registration is handled by [PKNIC](https://www.pknic.net.pk/), the .pk registry, under its own rules, so ask your developer to show you the PKNIC record in your name.

**Hosting.** The hosting account should be in your name, paid with your card or invoiced to you, with your email as the account owner. Your developer can be added as a user. Be wary of anyone who insists the site can only live on their server.

**Code and design files.** Agree in writing that you own the finished design and code once you have paid. This matters legally as well as practically. Under Pakistan's [Copyright Ordinance, 1962](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/3482), the starting point is that the author of a work is its first owner, and an assignment of copyright has to be in writing. Don't assume that paying for a website automatically makes it yours; put it in the contract. For larger projects, have a lawyer read that clause.

**Accounts.** Google Analytics, Search Console, Google Business Profile, Meta Business, payment gateway, email marketing: each should be created under your business email, with the developer given access rather than ownership.

As one example of how this can work, every Kelbari project ends with the client owning every file, repository and account. Whoever you hire, ask for the same, in writing.

## Contracts, milestones and payments

You don't need a long contract, but you do need a written one. At a minimum it should cover:

- **Scope:** the pages, features and integrations, ideally attached as the brief.
- **What's excluded:** copywriting, photography, product uploads, paid licences, hosting.
- **Revisions:** how many rounds at each stage, and what counts as a round.
- **Change requests:** how new ideas are priced once work has started.
- **Timeline:** dates for each milestone, and what happens if either side is late (including you, if content is late).
- **Payment schedule:** linked to milestones you can see.
- **Ownership and handover:** as above.
- **Support:** what's included after launch and for how long.

A typical milestone structure looks like this:

| Milestone | What you see | Payment |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Signed scope and timeline | Deposit |
| Design approved | Page designs on screen, before code | Second part |
| Staging site ready | Working site on a private link | Third part |
| Launch and handover | Live site, all logins and files in your name | Final part |

The exact split is a matter for negotiation. The principle is that you never pay for something you can't yet see, and the final payment is due when ownership is handed over, not before.

## Communication and process

Ask how the work will run, week by week. Good answers are specific:

- **A scoping call and a written brief** before any price. At Kelbari, for instance, the scoping call ends with a written brief, and a proposal usually follows within 48 hours.
- **Design reviewed before code.** It is much cheaper to change a layout on screen than after it has been built.
- **A staging link.** A private copy of the site that updates as work progresses, so you can check progress yourself rather than waiting for a big reveal. We share weekly builds this way, and it means problems surface early.
- **One named contact** and agreed channels: WhatsApp for quick questions, email for decisions you need on record.
- **Clear response times**, especially if you are in a different time zone from the developer.

## After-launch support

Websites need ongoing care. Patchstack's [State of WordPress Security in 2026](https://patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2026/) counted 11,334 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem in 2025, most of them in plugins. Updates are not optional.

Ask:

- What is included free after launch, and for how long (bug fixes, small text changes)?
- Who applies software and plugin updates, and how often?
- Where are backups stored, how often are they taken, and have you ever tested a restore?
- How are urgent problems reported, and how quickly are they handled?
- What does ongoing support cost, and can I cancel it?
- Will I get written handover notes: how to edit pages, where everything is hosted, which licences renew and when?

A monthly support arrangement is reasonable. One you can't leave without losing your website is not.

## Questions to ask a web design agency

Take this list to every conversation:

1. Can I see three live sites you built, and speak to one of those clients?
2. What exactly did you do on each of those projects?
3. Which platform do you recommend for my site, and why that one rather than another?
4. Will the domain, hosting, code and all accounts be in my name?
5. Which paid themes, plugins or services will my site need, and what do they cost each year?
6. Who will work on my project, and who is my day-to-day contact?
7. Will I see designs before you build, and a staging site before launch?
8. How many revision rounds are included, and how are extra changes priced?
9. What is the payment schedule?
10. What happens after launch: updates, backups, fixes, and what they cost?
11. How will you make the site fast on mobile, and how will you check it?
12. If we stop working together, what do I receive?

If your site needs to be found in search, Google's own guidance on [hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) suggests further questions, and our [SEO guide for small businesses](/blog/seo-for-small-businesses/) covers the basics a new site should have.

## Red flags when hiring a web development company in Pakistan

- **Guaranteed first-page rankings.** Google says [no one can guarantee a #1 ranking](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo).
- **Domain or hosting in the developer's name**, or a refusal to put them in yours.
- **No written scope**, just a price.
- **Full payment upfront** for anything beyond a very small job.
- **No live examples**, only screenshots or mock-ups.
- **"Lifetime" premium plugins** at a fraction of their price, which usually means unlicensed copies without updates.
- **Vague answers about who does the work**, or a project that seems to pass through several hands.
- **Pressure to decide today** with a discount that expires.

You can also check a company's registration. The SECP offers a [company name search](https://www.secp.gov.pk/company-name-search/) for incorporated companies. Many good freelancers and small studios are sole proprietors or partnerships, though, so not appearing there is not a red flag on its own.

## Questions

### How do I check if a web developer is reliable?

Look at live sites they built, run a couple through PageSpeed Insights, and speak to a past client. Ask for a written scope and milestone payments. Reliable developers are comfortable with all of this.

### Should I hire a freelancer or an agency?

A freelancer suits a small, clearly defined site. An agency suits projects that need design, development, content and marketing together, or where you can't afford to be stuck if one person becomes unavailable.

### Who should own my domain and hosting?

You should. Your business should be the registrant on the domain, and the hosting account should be in your name, with the developer added as a user.

### Is it safe to pay a developer in advance?

A deposit is normal. Paying in full before you have seen designs or a working staging site is not. Tie each payment to something you can see.

### What should I get at handover?

Admin logins for the site, ownership of the domain, hosting and every account, the source code or a full backup, design files, a list of paid licences with renewal dates, and short notes on how to edit the site.
